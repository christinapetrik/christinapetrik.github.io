Current Research

If you build it, will they come? Incentivizing Learning and Networks in Digital Financial Services

Working Paper

Despite the potential positive impacts of digital financial services (DFS) on household welfare, adoption rates remain low in some of the world's poorest countries. A key question is whether households face behavioral, information or network frictions that marginal incentives can overcome, and how these interact with uneven supply. To investigate this, we conducted a randomized controlled trial in Niger to estimate the effect of information provision and a two-sided financial incentive on DFS adoption, usage and welfare. We further integrate spatial data on agent networks to assess the role of supply-side constraints on adoption. We find that relaxing information constraints increases awareness and only induces adoption for wealthier households in the short-term. By contrast, a modest financial incentive provided to both the receiver and sender of a digital transfer increases both adoption and usage by 200-500%, which persisted over time. This also led to greater savings via mobile money, a greater likelihood of receiving transfers and higher subjective well-being. We develop a model of learning by doing with network effects to disentangle the mechanisms behind these results, and find suggestive evidence for both channels.

Parched Prospects: Assessing Farmers’ Climate Adaptation Strategies in the Wake of a Drought in Northern Ghana

Working Paper

Farmers in Sub-Saharan Africa face increasingly frequent weather shocks. A large literature documents their adverse effects on agricultural production and rural livelihoods, but less is known about how differences in weather shock severity shape farmers’ immediate production responses and forward-looking adaptation plans. Therefore, using panel data on soybean farmers in northern Ghana, we apply a difference-in-differences design to assess the impacts of an exceptionally severe drought. Our findings show that greater drought severity substantially reduces soybean yields and revenues, but farmers cut labor costs deliberately or because they do not cultivate at all. Higher drought severity is also associated with a higher likelihood to plan short- and long-term adaptation, primarily driven by plans directly within agriculture such as improving or changing agricultural technologies or practices.
